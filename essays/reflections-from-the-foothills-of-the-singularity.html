Reflections

from the foothills of The Singularity
November 18, 2026

When I’ve been asked about five-year+ career plans recently, I’ve often gone down some branch of a tree of futures that I haven’t fully explored or thought through. That does me a disservice, because it doesn’t convey the context in my head about why I’m saying what I’m saying. So here's an attempt to disentangle some of these thoughts and what I think they mean.


I’ve always tried to think carefully about where things are headed (in the context of AI/robotics), and about which breakthroughs and technological bottlenecks will shape how they get there. I think the best anyone can do is keep making these estimates and use them to plan their local trajectories. Over the last year or so, certain events have led me to make a few modest updates to my priors.


The first was due to how well multi-agent systems work, and the new axis of inference-time compute scaling they unlock. This unlock is behind most of this year’s landmark moments (Navier-Stokes, the Hugging Face attack, etc.). The second was how far models can go without spatiotemporal backbones. With fast compute, strong visual reasoning, contact sheets, images, and tool use, they handle much more temporal reasoning in domains like computer use & robotics than I expected. I still think fine-grained motor control requires a model to have a more native understanding of the temporal dimension (and a different type of output head).


However, seeing what these systems can do without video (and similar radar/sensory-temporal) encoders makes me increasingly bullish long-term on what agents plus video encoders (plus action decoder heads, and so on) could do in robotics. Moreover, tasks that can afford to have higher latency - where the model can afford to reason & use tools to fetch, zoom & manipulate images on the fly - will be increasingly done best by agents and follow a sustained price / performance boost.


The update that matters most for my personal plans is related to the pursuit for an automated AI research engineer. I’ve watched software engineering change over the last 12-15 months - many things I spent a lot of time learning, that used to matter, no longer do. Not all of them, of course- many of those skills still matter. Being able to understand the natural selection process occurring in software engineering is important to have expectations for how it’s going to continue to evolve the field (and others).


METR’s recent findings on AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic point in a similar direction for ML engineering, an area that I’ve historically spent a lot of time trying to get better at via work and reading research papers. I now put roughly a 50% probability on AI systems substantially automating AI research and engineering within 3 years, down from 5 years previously. That’s daunting, and it’s reshaping how I think about which skills will matter.


It’s daunting for other reasons too. If we reach the point where we’re blind to the training runs triggered by agents the way many of us have become blind to the code (and the neurons), what will the agents do?


I’ve previously talked about the model capability overhang that exists due to the underexploration of the model’s action space. “Capabilities” are broad and can be both negative and positive. There is likely a misalignment overhang as well: we haven’t yet discovered all the different ways that models can be misaligned, and both the magnitude of the risk and the size of the overhang grow with each model iteration. We know that frontier models have shown signs of reward-seeking behavior, spoofed tool calls to trick human monitors, and sandbagged when aware that they were undergoing safety evaluations. As models get better at these misaligned behaviors, detecting them will likely become increasingly difficult.


It seems as though the free market is steering the ship towards automated AI research and attempts at starting an intelligence explosion. It is possible for intentional slowdowns to occur due to cooperation between labs, governments & organizations, but I find it hard to believe that a setup like this could sustain itself unless the competitive dynamics of the race to AGI change from a setup that seems zero-sum to a positive-sum one. As long as labs are competing for market share of existing industries to justify their valuations, they will inevitably find themselves pushing for higher capabilities and recursive learning loops are one of the most tempting ways to do this.


While keeping pace with progress on the capability frontier can feel hopeless, I think now is the most important time for us to develop scientific means of investigating the actions and capabilities of models. Evaluating and validating these models and building holistic safety cases for them are of the utmost importance.
